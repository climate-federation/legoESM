\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Earth system models (ESMs) have grown enormously in realism and process complexity, yet
persistent biases and a stubbornly wide spread in the climate response to greenhouse-gas
forcing remain, and the dominant modelling paradigm makes it hard to adopt three
transformative tools: machine learning, gradient-based calibration, and accelerator
hardware. Most ESMs are written in Fortran or C, so machine-learning components are
implemented through one-way bridges that preclude online training; the absence of model
adjoints forces derivative-free tuning; and only isolated components have been ported to
GPUs. Further, the model hierarchy has driven much of our climate understanding, yet at
present a separate model is used at each complexity level, without a single unifying
infrastructure permitting clear hypothesis testing. Finally, models are built for either
weather or climate time scales. We present legoESM, an Earth system model developed from
scratch with AI agents and written entirely in JAX, in which every component (atmosphere,
ocean, land, sea ice, coupler) is composable, with dynamical cores, physics schemes, grids and
complexity levels swapping as interchangeable bricks, end-to-end differentiable, and
hardware-portable (CPU, GPU, TPU). The same code base spans the hierarchy of models, across
space and time scales, from large-eddy and cloud-resolving simulation through single-column
and idealized cases to global atmosphere and ocean integrations, spanning weather to climate
time scales. We demonstrate the flexibility of the model parameterizations against a
cloud-resolving oracle, tuning classical physics or column neural networks coupled to a
differentiable dynamical core with a spherical neural operator emulating the entire
atmosphere, global gradient-based calibration of land-surface temperature and albedo bias,
and grid-swapping validation against established models (NEMO, FV3) across cubed-sphere,
lat--lon, icosahedral/Voronoi and tripolar grids. legoESM was developed almost entirely by AI
coding agents working under a human-specified scientific contract, with mechanical
verification of every physical invariant through systematic benchmarking. It is intended to
provide an open, community-based playground for rapid prototyping, hypothesis testing,
research and teaching.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}
% =====================================================================
Earth system models have improved substantially over recent decades, both in their fidelity with
respect to the observed climate and in the breadth of processes they represent\cite{Eyring2016,IPCC2021}.
Yet important biases persist, so that the uncertainty in the response of the climate system to
greenhouse-gas forcing remains large and only loosely constrained by the historical record.
Equilibrium climate sensitivity is still uncertain at the $\sim$50\% level\cite{Sherwood2020};
projections of rainfall extremes\cite{OGorman2015}, of ocean heat uptake, and of the strength of
the land carbon sink\cite{Arora2020,Friedlingstein2022} differ substantially across models and are
often inconsistent across different regions of the world. Further, climate models have to a large
extent not embraced the data revolution: only a fraction of the wealth of observations and
observational products (satellites, reanalyses) is used for model calibration and evaluation. This
is often a reflection of the difficulty of using modern AI methods, especially gradient-based ones,
for model calibration.

Three developments could, in principle, reduce these uncertainties, but each is held back by the
prevailing software paradigm. First, machine learning (ML) has been used to emulate or replace
expensive parameterizations such as deep convection\cite{Gentine2018,Rasp2018,BrenowitzBretherton2018,YuvalOGorman2020}
and ocean mesoscale eddies\cite{BoltonZanna2019,ZannaBolton2020}, often improving key processes such
as rainfall extremes and eddy fluxes, and demonstrably reducing systematic errors in cloud cover,
precipitation and circulation\cite{IglesiasSuarez2024,Grundner2025,Heuer2026}. But because ESMs are
typically written in Fortran or C, ML components are coupled through one-way bridges that feed a
neural-network prediction into the physics model\cite{Ott2020}. As a result, online training, that is
learning through the model's own evolution, is generally impossible, and the resulting hybrid systems
are prone to instability\cite{BrenowitzBretherton2018} and cannot be further tuned to observations.
Second, many modelling centres have recently focused on automated tuning and calibration of uncertain
parameters\cite{Hourdin2017,Bonnet2025,Grundner2025}, given the growing complexity of the models, but
because the models typically do not have an adjoint (a gradient with respect to the parameters),
calibration relies on derivative-free Monte-Carlo or ensemble methods\cite{Cleary2021,Couvreux2021,Schneider2017}
that scale poorly with the number of parameters. Third, only a handful of models or components have
been ported to GPUs\cite{Fuhrer2018,Bauer2021}, and this remains the exception rather than the norm.
This has limited the capability to go to higher resolution to better resolve key processes such as
deep convection\cite{Eyring2024}.

A separate, long tradition in climate science is the concept of a hierarchy of models, spanning
idealized to complex configurations\cite{Held2005,Jeevanjee2017,Maher2019}. This hierarchy has been
fundamental to building physical understanding of the climate system. Today, however, different
classes of problems are addressed with different, largely incompatible codes and models, so there is
no unified framework in which to test hypotheses systematically across scales with clean
counterfactuals.

In parallel, a second revolution has transformed weather forecasting: data-driven models trained on
decades of reanalysis now rival or surpass physics-based forecasts at a tiny fraction of their
computational cost\cite{Pathak2022,Bi2023,Lam2023}. Yet purely learned forecasts tend to drift and
lose physical realism at long lead times\cite{Lehmann2026}, and on their own they do not provide the
conserved, multi-component system that climate projection demands. More fundamentally,
machine-learning emulators, however skilful and fast, reproduce input--output relationships without
representing the mechanisms that generate them, which limits their value as scientific instruments.
Earth system models are not merely predictive tools but research tools: their capacity for hypothesis
testing and mechanistic attribution is central to scientific progress. Preserving that capability
while gaining the computational and optimization advantages of differentiable, ML-enhanced methods is
the central motivation for process-based, differentiable Earth system models rather than pure
emulation.

Finally, all current Earth system models are based on languages (Fortran, C, Julia) that are not
widely known by junior scientists. This inherently limits adoption, progress and rapid model
evolution. The recent weather AI revolution has demonstrated that it is not only model performance
that drives widespread adoption, but also ease of use. The confluence of large-scale datasets,
GPU-accelerated computing, and differentiable programming frameworks capable of scaling
gradient-based optimization enabled the machine-learning revolution; Earth system science is now
positioned to leverage the same convergence, but doing so requires Earth system models written in
modern, automatically differentiable languages that can natively use GPUs, support gradient-based
optimization, and interface directly with machine learning.

We argue that a modern ESM should be (i) easy to use, ideally written in Python, the lingua franca of
junior scientists; (ii) hardware-agnostic, running on CPUs, GPUs and TPUs as accelerators evolve;
(iii) composable, so that different numerical methods, physical parameterizations or entire modules
(for example the whole atmosphere) can be swapped, combined and trained jointly for controlled
experiments; (iv) able to integrate AI seamlessly, whether by replacing a single parameterization or
an entire component (for example swapping the whole atmosphere for a neural network, as in recent AI
weather models\cite{Pathak2022,Bi2023,Lam2023,Kochkov2024}), which requires the code to be end-to-end
differentiable so it can be trained on diverse data with modern ML optimizers; (v) solvable on many
grids tailored to the problem at hand; and (vi) able to run across scales in large-eddy (LES),
cloud-resolving (CRM), regional or global configurations, bridging spatial scales as well as temporal
scales from weather to long-term climate.

A growing ecosystem of differentiable geoscientific models has begun to demonstrate these ideas,
though so far one component or one scale at a time. For the atmosphere, differentiable dynamical
cores and intermediate-complexity models such as the JAX Circulation Model\cite{Davenport2026}, the
neural general circulation model NeuralGCM\cite{Kochkov2024} and SpeedyWeather.jl\cite{Klower2024}
expose gradients for calibration, sensitivity analysis and online learning. For the ocean, JAX and
automatic-differentiation efforts, from Veros\cite{Hafner2018} and jaxsw\cite{Johnson2023jaxsw} to
program-transformation approaches to differentiable ocean modelling\cite{Moses2026DJ4Earth}, bring the
same capability to circulation and state estimation. For the land surface and hydrology,
differentiable models such as JAX-CanVeg\cite{Jiang2025CanVeg} and differentiable process-based
hydrology\cite{Shen2023} train physical parameters and neural components end to end against
observations, building on the broader case for differentiable programming in the
geosciences\cite{Gelbrecht2023,Kochkov2021}. What no framework has yet provided is a single model that
carries these properties across components, grids and scales at once, for the entire Earth system,
rather than a differentiable treatment of one piece in isolation.

Here we present legoESM. Rather than a single Earth system model, it is an open, modular framework in
which differentiable representations of individual processes can be assembled, coupled and
benchmarked against alternative formulations to address diverse scientific questions, conceived from
scratch as an evolving, agile, community-based infrastructure for testing ideas and hypotheses across
weather and climate (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}). Distinctively, it was developed almost entirely with AI
coding agents (Claude and Codex) operating under a human-authored scientific specification, with every
declared physical invariant checked mechanically through contracts, faithfulness oracles and
systematic benchmarking. We believe that legoESM can transform the way we do research and educate on
weather and climate.

\section*{A model assembled from interchangeable bricks}
% =====================================================================
legoESM was developed from scratch, in Python and JAX, using AI coding agents. It is built from
interchangeable building blocks, or ``bricks'' (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}): each component can be
developed, installed and run on its own, or combined with the others into a fully coupled model.
Concretely, it is a \emph{federation} of independently installable Python packages that share a
single \texttt{legoesm} namespace and obey a strict one-way dependency graph. At the base sits the substrate, \texttt{legoesm-core}: physical
constants, thermodynamics, the grid and operator library, state containers, time integrators,
the device/precision runtime, and the parallel layer (halo exchange and reductions). Four
Earth-system components (\texttt{atmosphere}, \texttt{ocean}, \texttt{land} and
\texttt{ice}) depend only on the core and never on one another, so each can be installed and
run on its own (\texttt{pip install legoesm-ocean} yields a working ocean model). Above the
components, an orchestration layer provides the tile-based \texttt{coupler} and drivers, the
\texttt{ml} package (spherical neural operators, learned physics, differentiable training and
variational data assimilation), and \texttt{tools} (external forcing, diagnostics with
conservation gates, and visualization). The whole stack is a pure pytree, so JAX's function
transformations (\texttt{jit}, \texttt{grad}, \texttt{vmap}, \texttt{scan}) apply through the
complete coupled model. Writing the model in JAX, rather than a lower-level language, secures two
key properties at once: identical code runs natively on CPUs, GPUs and TPUs and will compile to
whatever accelerators emerge next, sidestepping the multi-year effort of retrofitting a legacy
model to new hardware; and the entire coupled model is differentiable, so gradients are obtained
automatically without a hand-written adjoint.

The framework is built on four independently varying axes (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). The \textbf{grid} (cubed-sphere C-D,
lat--lon C-grid, Gaussian/spectral, Voronoi/MPAS, and tripolar) is chosen by one factory and
shared by every component. The \textbf{model complexity} runs along physical ladders
(shallow-water $\rightarrow$ hydrostatic $\rightarrow$ nonhydrostatic atmosphere; fixed
sea-surface temperature (SST) $\rightarrow$ slab $\rightarrow$ multi-layer $\rightarrow$ full
three-dimensional ocean; single-column model (SCM), large-eddy simulation (LES) and
cloud-resolving model (CRM)), so that one framework spans scales from metre-scale turbulence to
the global circulation, orthogonal to the numerical discretization. The \textbf{physics} is composable:
each component sums the tendencies of any chosen subset of process parameterizations, with
$\texttt{combined}=\sum_i\texttt{process}_i$ guaranteed additive to machine precision by tests.
The \textbf{fidelity} ranges from research-idealized to operational, following the standard
atmosphere-only, ocean-only and coupled model intercomparison protocols (AMIP, OMIP and CMIP),
encoded as test tiers that each add a stricter conservation gate. Mass is conserved by construction; energy
and momentum are tracked diagnostically with explicit budget closure. The model currently
provides more than twenty dynamical cores across five discretization families and more than
thirty physics parameterizations (Supplementary Information, Tables~S1--S3), together with a
3D Boussinesq ocean, a multi-layer land model with carbon cycle and multi-category sea ice. The
ocean and land span degrees of complexity from slab and multi-layer slabs to a full
three-dimensional Boussinesq ocean and a full Richards-equation land model, with or without a
carbon cycle. The framework is modular by design, so entire modules (for example the atmosphere)
can be replaced by machine learning, such as a spherical Fourier neural operator (SFNO), providing
a ladder from a fully physics-based configuration to fully emulated components. Full governing equations and
numerics are given in Supplementary Information~\S1. The source is openly available at
\url{\repourl}.

\paragraph{Development by AI agents under a scientific contract.}
legoESM was built through a human--agent workflow we summarise here because it is integral to
the result. We first used an agentic coding environment to define the framework and a written
specification (a project ``constitution'') stating the vision, the conserved quantities, the
sign and unit conventions, the valid sets of schemes, the reference literature, and the
acceptance criteria for each component: the human dictates the \emph{logic}; the agent fills
the \emph{body}; and every declared invariant is checked \emph{mechanically} so that violations
fail loudly. Agents then wrote small-scale unit tests covering most of the code, followed by a
ladder of increasingly stringent scientific benchmarks: standard dynamical-core tests of
rising complexity (shallow-water Williamson and Galewsky cases, the Jablonowski--Williamson
baroclinic wave, Held--Suarez, and DCMIP-style cases); parameterizations exercised in
radiative--convective equilibrium (RCE) coupled to ocean or land; and finally full
atmosphere-only and ocean-only integrations compared against established models such as the
NEMO ocean model and the GFDL FV3 atmospheric core. Boundary-layer
turbulence was validated against canonical LES cases (GABLS1, the Ekman layer and Wangara);
convection schemes were evaluated and then tuned against cloud-resolving models; and
microphysics was tested in LES and CRM runs against radiative--convective-equilibrium
intercomparison (RCEMIP) oracles. Weak and strong scaling were iterated on by the agents, with runs
on the Derecho and Levante supercomputers, and performance on multi-node message-passing (MPI) and
GPUs was improved iteratively; for the hardest problems, such as multi-node scaling, grid-imprint
artifacts at cubed-sphere panel edges and AMIP faithfulness, we used a persistent ``Ralph'' iteration
loop\footnote{The ``Ralph'' technique runs an agent repeatedly on the same task, feeding test
and diagnostic results back each cycle, until a target criterion is met
(\url{https://ghuntley.com/ralph/}).} to keep the agent working until the artifacts were
eliminated. Throughout, an adversarial code-review
agent (``codex'') reviewed every substantial change, which accelerated convergence and reduced
defects; community agent-skill libraries were used in the final stages to reach operational
quality and to surface subtle bugs. The result is a $\sim$$10^5$-line code base whose physical
contracts are enforced by continuous-integration tripwires (Methods).

Two features of this workflow are worth emphasising, because they may matter more than legoESM
itself. First, it is fast: with agents handling implementation and testing, adding a new
parameterization or dynamical core, historically a months-to-a-year undertaking in an
established model, can shrink to days, compressing the loop between a scientific idea and a
tested, benchmarked implementation. Second, it is \emph{expert-in-the-loop}: the agents are
productive only because domain scientists specify the governing logic, conserved quantities and
acceptance criteria and adjudicate the physical choices at each step. Without that guidance, a
general-purpose coding agent would not, on its own, arrive at a coupled model of this scientific
complexity with defensible numerics.

\section*{Many grids, one set of equations}
% =====================================================================
A central design goal is that the governing equations be solvable on whichever grid best suits
the problem. legoESM implements shallow-water, hydrostatic primitive-equation and
nonhydrostatic compressible-Euler dynamics across the major grid families used in Earth system
modelling: the cubed-sphere and latitude--longitude finite-volume grids, Gaussian/spectral
transforms, and unstructured Voronoi (MPAS) meshes. The specific discretizations, transport
operators and their community lineages are listed in Supplementary Table~S1.
As an illustration, the same shallow-water equations solved on the cubed-sphere, lat--lon,
icosahedral and spectral grids give near-identical solutions across all four discretizations,
including for the propagating Rossby--Haurwitz wave (Williamson~6)\cite{Williamson1992}, and the
cubed-sphere C-D core reproduces the Jablonowski--Williamson baroclinic
wave\cite{JablonowskiWilliamson2006}. Removing a residual grid imprint at cube-panel boundaries,
visible only on the meridional wind and not in error norms, was one of the problems for which
the agentic ``Ralph'' iteration loop proved essential; a duo-grid treatment\cite{Mouallem2023}
eliminates it. The full cross-grid verification suite (cosine-bell advection, the Williamson set,
the baroclinic wave and convergence rates) is given in Supplementary Information~\S2.

\section*{Composability and differentiable calibration}
% =====================================================================
Swapping one physical scheme for another to assess its effect is a routine scientific need,
yet it is difficult in conventional climate models, in which parameterizations are interwoven
with the rest of the code. In legoESM, every parameterization presents the same
tensor-in/tendency-out interface, taking the atmospheric state and returning a tendency, so
schemes can be exchanged without touching the rest of the model. Figure~\ref{fig:convection} illustrates this for atmospheric
convection, the moist rising motions that produce most tropical rainfall and a long-standing
source of model error. Ten different convection schemes, spanning the mass-flux, adjustment
and stochastic families (Extended Data Table~2), are each run, unchanged, in a single atmospheric
column held in radiative--convective equilibrium (a small, well-understood testbed) and scored
against the temperature profile that theory predicts. They separate cleanly by skill
(Fig.~\ref{fig:convection}), recovering the expected ordering and exposing which ones fail to
reach equilibrium: an experiment that is cumbersome in a conventional model but a one-line
change here. The same mechanism can replace an entire component: a classical physics suite, a
column neural network, or a full spherical neural operator\cite{Bonev2023} (a learned
dynamical core) can occupy the same slot, and the ocean can run as a prescribed sea surface,
a simple slab, or a full three-dimensional model, all from one driver (Supplementary
Information~\S3).

Differentiability turns calibration into gradient-descent optimization, leveraging one of the key
backbones of the machine-learning revolution. Because gradients flow through the model via
\jaxgrad{}, parameters can be optimized directly against data with modern ML optimizers (Adam,
MUON), rather than by derivative-free search\cite{Hourdin2017,Cleary2021} that scales poorly in
high dimension.
Figure~\ref{fig:landcal} demonstrates this for the land surface: tuning per-plant-functional-type
surface parameters by gradient descent against ERA5\cite{Hersbach2020} mean-annual
skin temperature, under physical-bound constraints imposed through differentiable
transforms, reduces the global mean warm bias from $+1.09$ to $+0.30$\,K, with the largest
improvements over the previously too-warm arid and semi-arid land. The same machinery has been used to calibrate convection-scheme parameters against
cloud-resolving references, and to adjust a cloud-fraction critical-relative-humidity threshold
using gradients of the top-of-atmosphere shortwave and longwave fluxes so that the planetary
albedo falls in its observed $\sim$30\% range. The differentiable parallel layer makes this
work at scale: exact reverse-mode gradients flow through distributed halo exchange (via a custom
adjoint that swaps send/receive ranks) and through the global reductions used by the
mass-conservation fixers (Methods).

The same adjoint capability underpins variational data assimilation. A three-dimensional
variational (3D-Var) analysis is implemented for the atmosphere: the analysis increment is
obtained by differentiating the observation operator and the background term through the model,
and it extends to 4D-Var through the differentiable time-stepping kernel, with no separately
coded adjoint model. Because calibration, data assimilation and hybrid training all reduce to
taking gradients through the \emph{same} model, legoESM makes it natural to pose
questions that are cumbersome in conventional models, for example which processes a given bias is
most sensitive to. As a concrete example of the capability we most want to highlight, the gradient
of any scalar diagnostic $J$ (a global-mean radiative imbalance, a regional precipitation bias, a
measure of tropical variability) with respect to \emph{every} internal parameter and boundary
input is obtained in a single reverse pass, at a cost essentially independent of the number of
parameters; ranking those sensitivities attributes the diagnostic to the processes that control it,
where the brute-force equivalent would demand thousands of perturbed integrations. This shifts the
emphasis from demonstrating predictive skill to using the model
as an instrument for understanding.

\section*{Bridging scales and the model hierarchy, from turbulence to the globe}
% =====================================================================
Climate processes span an enormous range of scales, from turbulent eddies metres across to
circulations spanning the planet, and traditionally each scale is treated with a separate
model. legoESM covers this range within a single framework. On a small
doubly periodic domain, its nonhydrostatic core with a turbulence closure\cite{Smagorinsky1963}
performs large-eddy simulation, resolving the largest turbulent motions directly: the canonical
GABLS1 stable boundary layer\cite{Beare2006}, the Ekman layer, the
Wangara diurnal cycle, and the DYCOMS-II\cite{Stevens2005} and RICO\cite{vanZanten2011}
cloud-topped layers all reproduce the expected mean profiles and turbulence structure
(Supplementary Information~\S4). Enlarging the domain and adding moist physics turns the same
core into a cloud-resolving model: in radiative--convective equilibrium\cite{Wing2018} it
spontaneously organizes convection into clusters (``self-aggregation''), and its warm-rain microphysics matches a benchmark
particle-based reference\cite{Shima2009,Bartman2022} (Supplementary Information~\S4). Turbulence and microphysics schemes were additionally checked against well-regarded LES codes
(MicroHH) and the cloud-resolving configuration against SAM. At the global scale, the
cubed-sphere atmosphere runs prescribed-ocean (AMIP-style) simulations, evaluated with
ESMValTool\cite{Eyring2016}, and the three-dimensional ocean runs forced (OMIP-style)
integrations (next section). Spanning a
metre-scale eddy to a global simulation within one differentiable code base is, to our
knowledge, unique, and it is precisely what hybrid physics--AI modelling needs, since the
high-resolution end can generate training data and the global end can host the learned scheme.

% =====================================================================
\section*{Global simulations against established models}
% =====================================================================
A stringent test of the framework is whether its ocean component, built from the same
composable elements, can reproduce a mature community model on the complex grids used in
operational ocean modelling. The three-dimensional Boussinesq ocean uses a split-explicit baroclinic/barotropic
solver\cite{ShchepetkinMcWilliams2005} with KPP vertical mixing\cite{LMD1994} and GM--Redi eddy
parameterization\cite{GM1990}, and runs on latitude--longitude, cubed-sphere, unstructured
(MPAS/Voronoi) and NEMO-style tripolar grids.
Figure~\ref{fig:ocean} compares legoESM ocean integrations against
NEMO\cite{Madec2017} on two of the most demanding meshes. On the unstructured MPAS/Voronoi
icosahedral grid, the simulated sea-surface temperature and the zonal-mean temperature section
closely track NEMO across the global domain and the full water column
(Fig.~\ref{fig:ocean}a,b). On the tripolar grid, whose folded northern boundary is a classic
source of numerical artifacts, the modelled sea-surface height matches the NEMO field with a
spatial correlation of 0.95 and near-zero bias (Fig.~\ref{fig:ocean}c). A centennial spin-up
library, an AMOC tracker, conservation diagnostics, and a peer-comparison fidelity harness
support quantitative model intercomparison (Supplementary Information~\S5). That an
agent-built ocean reproduces a mature community model on its own native, geometrically awkward
grids is a strong test of the framework. The same drivers run the standard uncoupled
model-intercomparison protocols end to end. In atmosphere-only (AMIP) mode, the cubed-sphere
atmosphere is integrated with prescribed observed sea-surface temperatures and sea ice and
reproduces a realistic global climatology of near-surface temperature, precipitation and
large-scale circulation. In ocean-only (OMIP) mode, the three-dimensional ocean is driven by
prescribed surface fluxes and tracks NEMO, as shown above. In land-only (LMIP) mode, the land
model is forced by reanalysis meteorology; this is the configuration used for the differentiable
calibration of Fig.~\ref{fig:landcal}. All three are described, with their forcing and evaluation
metrics, in Supplementary Information~\S5.

\section*{Performance and scalability}
% =====================================================================
Long integrations and large ensembles demand efficiency on whatever hardware is at hand, and
writing legoESM in JAX delivers this portably, without hand-written accelerator code.
A single consumer GPU accelerates the atmosphere by $16$--$21\times$ and the ocean by
$33$--$99\times$
over a 24-core CPU at single precision (Supplementary Information~\S6), with structured grids
(lat--lon, cubed-sphere) outperforming unstructured (MPAS) and spectral cores, as expected from
memory-access patterns. Two algorithmic changes found during agentic optimization dominate the
gains: parallelizing the correlated-$k$ radiation over its $g$-points ($\sim$6$\times$ on the
radiation kernel) and a split-explicit barotropic ocean solver that escapes the global-reduction
latency wall of the implicit solver (up to $1.65\times$ faster at 64 ranks). Across MPI ranks
and devices the model scales well where communication is amortized (Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling}): the
ocean reaches near-ideal parallel efficiency at production tile sizes, and the icosahedral
atmosphere retains $0.69$ parallel efficiency at 32 ranks (relative to an 8-rank baseline).
Reverse-mode differentiability is
preserved across all of this: gradients flow correctly through MPI halo exchange and global
sum reductions, verified against single-rank references to machine precision (Methods). Honest
limits are documented (spectral transforms remain communication-bound, and cross-node
cube-panel exchange on a commodity TCP fabric anti-scales) and are reported in Supplementary
Information~\S6 rather than hidden.

\section*{Accessibility, physical consistency and reproducibility}
% =====================================================================
Because legoESM is written in idiomatic Python, an entire experiment is a few lines of readable
code (selecting a grid, a complexity rung, a set of physics schemes and a forcing), which lowers
the barrier for students and domain scientists (Supplementary Information~\S7). Physical
consistency is treated as a first-class, mechanically tested property rather than an aspiration.
Operators reproduce their analytic targets: the vector-invariant advection (Burgers), Laplacian
viscous decay, Coriolis and inertial-oscillation tendencies all match closed-form solutions, and
finite-volume transport and gravity-wave terms converge at their design orders under
CFL-controlled refinement (Supplementary Information~\S8). Every physics module declares a
machine-readable contract of its units, signs, conserved quantities and differentiability, and
continuous-integration tripwires reject any change that hard-codes a physical constant outside
the central registry, re-derives a saturation curve, silently drops a scheme on a typo, or
breaks additive composition (Methods). Every run emits a reproducibility manifest (resolved
configuration, state digest, git commit and library versions) so that a single command
re-executes it bit-for-bit.

\section*{Summary and way ahead}
% =====================================================================
legoESM shows that a single, high-level, differentiable code base can span the Earth-system model
hierarchy, from large-eddy and cloud-resolving simulation to global atmosphere and ocean
integrations, while remaining composable enough to swap dynamical cores, grids, physics schemes and
complexity levels as interchangeable bricks, and differentiable enough to calibrate parameters and
train embedded neural networks by gradient descent through the model. The single property
that makes learning inside the model possible, differentiability from end to end, also
delivers gradient-based calibration and exact data assimilation, drawing three previously
separate research programmes into one framework. Concretely, the architecture makes practical
several things that are awkward or impossible in a conventional model: differentiating a
whole-model diagnostic back to the parameter or input that controls it (end-to-end sensitivity
and attribution); training an embedded neural component online, through the model's own
evolution rather than on offline single-step tendencies; and running the identical code from a
large-eddy column to a global simulation for clean cross-scale comparison. These capabilities,
rather than any single simulation, are the novelty we wish to demonstrate.

Two broader points deserve emphasis. First, treating differentiability and composability as
architectural invariants, rather than features bolted onto an existing model, is what makes the
hierarchy continuous: the LES closure, the global parameterization and the neural operator are
the same kind of object and obey the same interface. Second, the model was built largely by AI
agents, but its trustworthiness rests on a human-authored scientific contract whose every
clause is checked mechanically; the agent supplies implementation effort, while conservation,
sign conventions, valid scheme sets and acceptance criteria are dictated and verified by people.
We found adversarial agent review and persistent iteration loops essential precisely for the
problems where physical intuition is hard to encode, such as grid-imprint artifacts at
cubed-sphere edges.

A third point concerns the role of benchmarks. Agentic development of legoESM was possible only
because the community has, over decades, distilled its understanding into open, well-posed
benchmark cases and datasets, from the standard dynamical-core tests to RCEMIP, GABLS and the
AMIP, OMIP and LMIP protocols, which gave the agents unambiguous targets against which every
component could be checked. This hints at a broader shift: where a model's source code was once
the primary repository of physical understanding, in an agentic workflow the code becomes
comparatively inexpensive to regenerate, and it is the tests, benchmarks and reference datasets,
the accumulated work of the whole community, that increasingly encode what we know and that must
be curated and preserved most carefully.

legoESM builds on the extraordinary body of physical understanding encoded in community models
developed over decades (from the parameterizations of the Community Earth System
Model\cite{Lawrence2019}, the ocean physics of NEMO\cite{Madec2017}, the dynamical cores
pioneered at GFDL, and many others; see Extended Data Tables~1--10), repackaged in a modern,
differentiable infrastructure. This paper is a demonstration of the potential of that
architecture rather than a finished climate model: the framework has not yet been evaluated in
long, fully coupled climate integrations, and so does not yet match the maturity of established
operational models. Two caveats deserve emphasis. Gradient-based calibration fits a chosen target
efficiently, but it does not remove structural error: a well-tuned configuration can still carry
the biases inherent in its scheme set, and sharpening one metric can worsen others. And while the
differentiable coupler makes assembling components straightforward, we anticipate the same hard
coupled-system problems that challenge established models, such as spurious deep-water formation
in the Labrador Sea, drift in the Atlantic meridional overturning circulation, and biases in
tropical interannual variability (ENSO); demonstrating skill on these is essential future work.
To make the framework usable and checkable by others, it ships a set of curated reference
configurations, the idealized dynamical-core tests, radiative--convective equilibrium, and the
AMIP, OMIP and LMIP set-ups shown here, each with expected results, so that a student or a new
group can reproduce a known-good baseline before modifying it. Framed this way, legoESM is
intended as an open, evolving research and educational tool, for theoretical insight, hypothesis
testing and clean counterfactual experiments, and, in time and with
sustained community contributions, potentially a full-fledged climate model. Such
differentiable, AI-ready infrastructure is a concrete step toward the hybrid Earth system models
that are increasingly seen as a route to sharper, more actionable climate
projections\cite{Eyring2024}. By making the full coupled system easy to read, easy to
differentiate and easy to run on modern hardware, we hope to lower the barrier to
experimentation and to invite the community to build on and contribute to it.

Finally, the strategy reaches beyond climate. The design choices underlying legoESM
(composability, differentiability, hardware portability, and agent-assisted development under a
human-defined scientific contract) are responses to structural features shared by many
multiscale physical systems: combustion and reactive flows, materials science, plasma physics,
astrophysical fluid dynamics, and biological transport all couple processes across vastly
different scales, all stand to benefit from gradient-based inverse methods, and all face the
same tension between legacy code and modern accelerators. A composable, differentiable,
agent-built framework in which domain scientists specify the governing logic, conserved
quantities and acceptance criteria while AI agents write and mechanically verify the
implementation offers a template for simulation-based science far more broadly, wherever
physical models are complex, data are abundant and trustworthiness must be earned rather than
assumed.

\section*{Methods}
% =====================================================================
\small
The complete governing equations and numerical methods of all components are given in
Supplementary Information~\S1; this section summarises the computational implementation.
End-to-end differentiability, conservation and stable-shape (non-retracing) JAX execution are
treated throughout as hard constraints.

\subsection*{Agentic development methodology}
legoESM was implemented by large-language-model coding agents in an agentic coding environment
under a human-authored specification. Most of the code was written by Claude (Anthropic; the Opus
and Sonnet model families) driving edits, test execution and debugging, and a second agent based
on Codex performed adversarial review of every substantial change. A human-maintained
specification fixed the conserved quantities, sign and unit conventions, valid scheme sets,
reference literature and per-component acceptance criteria, and domain scientists reviewed and
adjudicated the scientific choices at each step (expert-in-the-loop). For hard,
slowly-converging problems (multi-node scaling; cube-edge grid artifacts) a persistent ``Ralph''
loop re-ran the agent on the same task, feeding back test and diagnostic output until the
acceptance criterion was met; community agent-skill libraries (\texttt{agent-skills},
\texttt{superpowers}) were used in the final hardening stages. Physical contracts are enforced
mechanically by continuous-integration tripwires: constants come only from a central registry,
saturation curves only from the shared thermodynamics module, every scheme factory raises on an
unknown selection, and additive physics composition is asserted to machine precision, each
tripwire shipping a self-test that proves it is non-vacuous.

\paragraph{Use-case configurations.}
The convection sweep (Fig.~\ref{fig:convection}) runs each scheme, unchanged, in the 40-level
single-column model to a statistically steady radiative--convective equilibrium and scores the
mean temperature profile against the surface moist adiabat. The land calibration
(Fig.~\ref{fig:landcal}) optimizes per-plant-functional-type soil-water and surface parameters by
gradient descent (Adam\cite{KingmaBa2015}) against ERA5\cite{Hersbach2020} annual-mean skin
temperature, with physical bounds imposed by sigmoid transforms and gradients taken through the
differentiable land integration. Ocean comparisons (Fig.~\ref{fig:ocean}) integrate the
three-dimensional ocean on MPAS/Voronoi and tripolar grids against NEMO\cite{Madec2017}.

\paragraph{JAX implementation and differentiability.}
All components are pure-functional JAX, integrated in time with \texttt{lax.scan} and using
\texttt{jnp.where}/\texttt{lax.cond} for data-dependent branching so that the program is
differentiable and JIT-compilable. State objects and fields are registered as pytrees, so
\texttt{jit}, \texttt{grad}, \texttt{vmap} and \texttt{scan} apply through the whole coupled
model. Feature gates that must not trace both branches (e.g.\ conservation fixers) use a static
Python conditional in a closure. Because buffer donation conflicts with reverse-mode autodiff,
the compiled time-stepping kernel provides a non-donating variant for use inside
\texttt{eqx.filter\_value\_and\_grad}, and per-iteration forcings (insolation, SST) are passed
as explicit traced arguments rather than captured in closures to avoid recompilation.

\paragraph{Differentiable distributed execution.}
Distributed runs use \texttt{initialize\_distributed} to build the MPI topology, then scatter to
rank-local tiles, step, and gather only for I/O. Halo exchange uses a four-dimensional path that
sends all vertical levels in one message per neighbour. Reverse-mode gradients flow through
communication: every \texttt{sendrecv} is wrapped in a custom vector--Jacobian product that
swaps source and destination ranks in the backward pass, and the mass-conservation fixers
differentiate through \texttt{allreduce(SUM)}. Only summation reductions are AD-safe;
\texttt{max}/\texttt{min}/\texttt{allgather} are restricted to diagnostics. Multi-rank results
are verified against single-rank references to $\sim$$10^{-9}$, and gradients are checked the
same way.

\paragraph{Training and calibration.}
Three training modes share one differentiable segment kernel: physics-parameter tuning, neural
GCM, and SFNO-plus-dynamical-core. Trainable parameters are injected through the configuration
pytree inside the loss (so leaves are traced), while production keeps static Python-float leaves
that constant-fold without retracing. Physical bounds are imposed by sigmoid/softplus transforms.
Optimization uses warmup-plus-cosine schedules with gradient clipping (Adam\cite{KingmaBa2015} or
Muon\cite{Jordan2024Muon}); ERA5\cite{Hersbach2020} is ingested through a Zarr-cached loader. The
land calibration in Fig.~\ref{fig:landcal} optimizes per-plant-functional-type parameters
against ERA5 skin temperature; the convection calibration optimizes scheme parameters against a
cloud-resolving reference.

\paragraph{Verification harness.}
The human-authored specification is enforced by mechanical tripwires in continuous integration:
physical constants may come only from a central registry; saturation curves may come only from
the shared thermodynamics module; every scheme factory must raise on an unknown selection rather
than silently default; and additive physics composition is asserted to machine precision. Each
physics module declares a contract (units, signs, conserved quantities, differentiability,
reference, idealized test), and a self-test proves each tripwire is non-vacuous. Dynamical cores
are gated on the standard sphere test set\cite{Williamson1992,JablonowskiWilliamson2006,HeldSuarez1994};
spatial/grid artifacts additionally require visual regression because error norms are necessary
but not sufficient. Substantial changes additionally passed an adversarial code-review agent.

\paragraph{Configurations for the figures.}
Shallow-water tests use cubed-sphere C36, lat--lon $72\times144$, icosahedral level-5 and
spectral T21 grids (Supplementary Information~\S2). RCE convection sweeps
(Fig.~\ref{fig:convection}) use the single-column model with shared physics. LES and CRM cases
use the nonhydrostatic plane core, the latter following the RCEMIP RCE300 protocol\cite{Wing2018}
(Supplementary Information~\S4). Ocean comparisons (Fig.~\ref{fig:ocean}) use MPAS/Voronoi
(icosahedral) and tripolar (eORCA) grids against NEMO\cite{Madec2017}. Scaling was measured on a
single RTX-class GPU (throughput/speed-up) and on a CPU cluster with one process per node
(strong/weak scaling; Supplementary Information~\S6). Full settings are in Supplementary
Information~\S1--\S8.
