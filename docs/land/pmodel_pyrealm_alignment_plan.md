
# Towards a sub-daily, optimality-based photosynthesis scheme in legoESM

*Comparison of the legoESM P-model with pyrealm and the JULES P-model, and a
plan for its revision.*
Draft of 2026-11-06 (N. Kangari, with Claude). Working document on branch
`testing_pmodel`; nothing here is committed. Numbers come from scoping scripts
kept outside the repository (Appendix B); they will be regenerated by committed
scripts before they are quoted in a pull request.

---

## 1. Motivation

legoESM contains a P-model (Stocker et al., 2020) that predicts acclimated
photosynthetic traits from the environment instead of reading them from
plant-functional-type tables. Since that implementation was written, the
P-model has developed in three ways that matter for a land-surface model
running at an hourly or half-hourly step:

1. **Explicit separation of fast and slow responses.** Mengoli et al. (2022)
   showed that a P-model applied at sub-daily resolution must distinguish the
   instantaneous response of photosynthesis to light, temperature and humidity
   from the slow acclimation of the traits themselves (Vcmax, Jmax and the
   stomatal sensitivity ξ), which adjust over roughly two weeks to the
   conditions that prevail near midday. This is the formulation implemented in
   pyrealm's `SubdailyPModel` and in the JULES P-model developed by
   N. Kangari.
2. **A revised quantum yield.** Sandoval et al. (2026) found that the intrinsic
   quantum efficiency of photosynthesis, φ0, follows a peaked response to
   temperature whose optimum acclimates to the growth temperature, and that
   its maximum declines with aridity. This replaces both the fixed φ0 and the
   Bernacchi et al. (2003) quadratic.
3. **A maintained reference implementation.** pyrealm (Orme et al.) now
   provides the full P-model family, and the JULES branch provides an
   implementation of the same theory inside a land-surface model with a
   comparable time step.

The aim of this work is to bring legoESM's P-model to this formulation, as
opt-in options, and to document with site and global benchmarks what each
change does and why.

## 2. Theoretical background

### 2.1 The least-cost optimum for χ

The P-model assumes that plants minimise the summed costs of maintaining
carboxylation capacity and transpiration per unit carbon gain (Prentice et
al., 2014). The solution is an optimal ratio of leaf-internal to ambient CO2,

  χ = Γ*/ca + (1 − Γ*/ca) · ξ / (ξ + √D),  ξ = √[β (K + Γ*) / (1.6 η*)],

where D is the vapour pressure deficit, K the effective Michaelis–Menten
coefficient of Rubisco, Γ* the photorespiratory compensation point, η* the
viscosity of water relative to 25 °C, and β the ratio of the unit costs of
carboxylation and transpiration (β = 146 for C3; Stocker et al., 2020). ξ
increases with temperature, because K and Γ* rise faster than the viscosity
term falls, so warmer growth conditions favour a higher χ. The same
square-root dependence of the stomatal response on D appears in the
Medlyn et al. (2011) model, which is why a Medlyn slope g1 can be mapped
onto ξ.

### 2.2 Coordination and the Jmax cost

The coordination hypothesis (Chen et al., 1993; Maire et al., 2012) holds
that Rubisco capacity is adjusted so that carboxylation-limited and
electron-transport-limited rates are equal under typical daytime conditions,
because capacity beyond that point would cost nitrogen without increasing
carbon gain. Wang et al. (2017) added an explicit cost of maintaining Jmax
(c* = 0.41), which gives

  Vcmax = φ0 Iabs (m'/mc), Jmax = 4 φ0 Iabs / √[(1/L)² − 1],

with m and mc the CO2 limitation terms of the electron-transport and
carboxylation rates and L the Jmax-limitation factor. **Vcmax is
proportional to the absorbed light Iabs** at which the plant is assumed to
acclimate. That proportionality drives most of what follows.

### 2.3 The quantum yield

φ0 sets the slope of the light response and, through the coordination
equation, scales Vcmax and Jmax one-for-one. Three formulations are in use:

* **Temperature-dependent with a reference value.** φ0 = φ0,ref × (0.352 +
  0.022 T − 3.4×10⁻⁴ T²), the Bernacchi et al. (2003) response of PSII
  efficiency. Stocker et al. (2020) calibrated φ0,ref = 0.0818 against
  FLUXNET GPP for a *daily* model without soil-moisture stress; pyrealm and the
  JULES branch default to φ0,ref = 1/8, the theoretical value for a C3 leaf
  without a Q-cycle (Long et al., 1993). A calibrated φ0,ref absorbs structural
  biases of the model it was fitted in, so 0.0818 is not transferable to a
  sub-daily scheme without re-examination.
* **Sandoval et al. (2026).** φ0 = φ_peak(AI) × f(T; Tg), with a peak value of
  1/9 reduced by the climatological aridity index AI = PET/P, and a peaked
  Arrhenius temperature function whose optimum shifts with the mean growth
  temperature (mGDD0, mean daily temperature of days above 0 °C). The
  activation entropy follows a power law in mGDD0, which is undefined at
  mGDD0 ≤ 0 °C; the JULES implementation clamps mGDD0 to 1–50 °C.

  *How the growth temperature is obtained.* pyrealm does not compute it: the
  user supplies `mean_growth_temperature` (°C) with the forcing, bounded to
  0–50 °C but not clamped; in the sub-daily model it is averaged over the
  acclimation window like any other driver, which leaves a climatological
  constant unchanged. The JULES set-up computes mGDD0 offline
  (`jules_src/ancillaries/make_mgdd0_wfdei.py`) as a climatology: daily means
  of the 3-hourly WFDEI air temperature over UTC days, then the mean of all
  days with a daily mean above 0 °C over 1980–2010; points with no such day
  (Greenland, Arctic islands) are set to the 1 °C clamp. It agrees with
  site-based values at 170 flux sites (r = 0.999, bias −0.2 °C). For the site
  runs here we will apply the same definition to the site record (daily means
  over local days from the half-hourly forcing). Two caveats: the site records
  are short (four years), so mGDD0 is a short-period estimate, and the
  definition is taken from the JULES implementation; it should be confirmed
  against Sandoval et al. (2026) before the results are described. mGDD0 is
  a different quantity from the canopy's own growth temperature used in the
  Kattge & Knorr temperature responses (a running 10-day mean).

### 2.4 Two time scales of response

Mengoli et al. (2022) separate (i) the instantaneous response of assimilation
to the current light, temperature and VPD, given fixed traits, from (ii) the
acclimation of the traits, which follow the daily optimum computed for
conditions near midday with a memory of about 15 days (a daily weight
α = 1/15). The choice of midday is deliberate: traits are optimised for the
high-light conditions in which most carbon is fixed, not for the 24-hour
average that includes the night. The daily P-model of Stocker et al. (2020)
evaluates the optimum at daily-mean light. That is appropriate for daily GPP,
because P-model GPP is linear in absorbed light, but the Vcmax it implies is a
daily-average capacity rather than the leaf's actual capacity at midday.

### 3.1 Why legoESM's Vcmax is about half that of the sub-daily scheme

Fed the same weather and the same light, the current legoESM scheme predicts
summer Vcmax25 at 0.49–0.57 of the sub-daily value at four flux sites
(§4.2). Three mechanisms contribute, and their signs can be predicted from
§2:

1. **Light.** Because Vcmax ∝ Iabs, sizing capacity on the 24-hour mean light
   rather than the midday light reduces it in proportion. At the four sites
   the 24-hour mean absorbed light in summer is 0.44–0.47 of the noon-window
   mean, which alone would lower Vcmax by a factor of 2.1–2.3.
2. **Temperature.** The 24-hour mean temperature is lower than the midday
   temperature. A cooler acclimation temperature lowers K and Γ* and therefore
   ξ, which lowers χ and the m'/mc ratio (§2.1–2.2); it also changes the
   normalisation of Vcmax to 25 °C. These effects partly offset the light
   effect, giving the net factor of 1.8–2.0.
3. **Where the memory acts.** Smoothing the drivers and then optimising, or
   optimising daily and then smoothing the traits, differ only through the
   non-linearity of the optimum over a 15-day window. Because the optimum is
   linear in light and only weakly non-linear in temperature over that
   window, the difference is small (≤ 3 %).

The light basis, by contrast, is not a cause: legoESM's sunlit-leaf absorbed
PAR is within 8 % of the JULES top-leaf light (k·PPFD with k = 0.5) at noon.
Both are leaf-level quantities, and for a spherical leaf-angle distribution a
sunlit leaf intercepts about half the incident beam per unit leaf area.
pyrealm's native fAPAR·PPFD is a canopy total per ground area and is
correspondingly about 1.8 times larger in closed canopies. It would be
inconsistent with a two-leaf canopy that already distributes capacity with
depth.

### 3.2 Why current legoESM GPP is nevertheless close to observations

Despite capacities that are about half those of the theory's midday
optimum, the current least-cost setting reproduces observed GPP at the four
sites with small bias (−0.8 to +0.2 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, §4.3). This points to
compensating structure elsewhere in the canopy rather than to a correct Vcmax.
Candidate compensations, to be tested rather than assumed:

* **The light response of the canopy kernel.** CLM's electron-transport
  curve (θ = 0.7, I_PSII = 0.5 × 0.85 × APAR) implies an initial quantum
  efficiency of about 0.106 mol C per mol photon, independent of temperature.
  That is higher than the P-model's φ0 = 0.0818 × f(T) used to size Vcmax.
  Light-limited assimilation, which dominates the shaded leaves and the
  morning and evening hours, is therefore governed by a different φ0 from the
  one that set the traits.
* **Inconsistent Jmax.** The Wang et al. (2017) optimum for Jmax assumes the
  light response J = 4φ0I/√[1 + (4φ0I/Jmax)²]. The kernel uses a
  non-rectangular hyperbola with a different curvature, so the "optimal" Jmax
  is not optimal for the response that consumes it.
* **Soil-moisture stress.** The empirical β(θ) multiplies both Vcmax and the
  stomatal slope and may hold GPP down where a larger capacity would be
  realised.

If these compensations are real, introducing the theoretically consistent
midday acclimation could increase GPP beyond observations. That outcome would
not argue against the change. It would identify the compensating error, which
should then be corrected rather than retained.

### 3.3 ci and stomatal conductance

In legoESM the P-model does not prescribe ci. It supplies a Medlyn slope
chosen so that the Medlyn model reproduces the P-model χ at the acclimation
conditions; ci then emerges from the coupled solution of assimilation,
conductance and leaf energy balance for sunlit and shaded leaves. At midday
in summer the solved ci/ca is within 0.02 of the P-model χ at the three mesic
sites but 0.07 higher at the Mediterranean savanna (US-Ton). A likely reason,
still to be confirmed, is the minimum conductance g0: when soil-moisture
stress suppresses assimilation, a fixed g0 becomes a large fraction of gs and
pushes ci/ca towards 1. The P-model's χ has no such floor. Leaf-surface
rather than air VPD, and the Γ*/ca term that Medlyn's form omits, contribute
smaller differences. The JULES branch and pyrealm instead set χ directly
from the lagged ξ and the current VPD; that option is planned (§5, step E).

### 3.3b Scaling from the top leaf to the canopy

The light that sizes the traits is the same in legoESM and the JULES runs
(sunlit-leaf absorbed PAR vs k·PPFD, within 8 %; §4.2b), but the scaling of
leaf capacity and assimilation to the canopy is not.

* **JULES** (`can_rad_mod = 1`, `l_pmodel_kscale`) is a big-leaf canopy:
  the top-leaf net assimilation (min(Ac, Aj) at the top-leaf capacity and
  light) is multiplied by (1 − e^(−kL))/k with k = 0.5
  (`sf_stom_jls_mod.F90`, `fpar`, `anetc = anetl * fpar`). This assumes that
  capacity and absorbed light decline with depth at the same rate, so every
  layer is in the top leaf's limitation state. It is the canopy form of the
  coordination hypothesis, and it reproduces the P-model's canopy result:
  k·PPFD · (1 − e^(−kL))/k = fAPAR·PPFD.
* **legoESM** is a two-leaf canopy (de Pury & Farquhar 1997; Ryu et al.
  2011): sunlit and shaded leaves each receive their own absorbed PAR from
  the two-stream transfer and their own share of capacity, and their FvCB
  rates are summed. Capacity declines with depth through a nitrogen profile
  whose coefficient comes from the site LAI (kn = 0.98 − 0.62 ln LAI, DifferBESS),
  much shallower than the light profile.

Canopy-integrated Vcmax per unit top-leaf Vcmax (summer LAI):

| Site (LAI)   | legoESM, site kn | JULES big leaf, k = 0.5 | ratio | legoESM `coordination` profile, f = 1 |
| ------------ | ---------------- | ----------------------- | ----- | --------------------------------------- |
| DE-Hai (5.2) | 4.77             | 1.85                    | 2.6   | 1.79                                    |
| DE-Obe (4.0) | 3.64             | 1.73                    | 2.1   | 1.97                                    |
| US-MMS (5.5) | 5.04             | 1.87                    | 2.7   | 2.14                                    |
| US-Ton (1.1) | 0.85             | 0.86                    | 1.0   | 0.86                                    |

In the closed canopies legoESM carries 2.1–2.7 times the capacity JULES does
for the same top-leaf Vcmax25; in the open savanna the two agree. Most of
that extra capacity sits in shaded, light-limited leaves, so GPP does not
scale with it, but it makes canopy GPP more sensitive to the light-limited
rate and hence to φ0. legoESM already has a profile consistent with the
coordination hypothesis (`canopy_vcmax_profile = 'coordination'`, capacity
following the light envelope; with `vcmax_light_frac = 1` it is within 0–15 %
of the JULES scaling above). Its default fraction of 0.35 reflects
observations that nitrogen and capacity gradients are shallower than light
gradients (de Pury & Farquhar 1997; Niinemets), so neither profile is
uncontested.

### 3.4 Smaller, documented departures

* **Pressure dependence of Kc and Ko.** Rubisco's Michaelis constants are
  properties of the dissolved-gas concentration and are conventionally fixed
  partial pressures (rpmodel, pyrealm, JULES). legoESM follows CLM in
  scaling them with surface pressure, which lowers K by 10–22 % at 70 kPa
  (about 3 km) and, through ξ and χ, Vcmax by 3–19 %. The difference is
  negligible below about 1 km. The CLM convention is retained for now (user
  decision) and noted as a likely bias over high-elevation terrain.
* **Normalisation to 25 °C.** legoESM converts the growth-temperature optimum
  to 25 °C with the same Kattge & Knorr (2007) response its canopy kernel uses
  to convert back, so the operational capacity is unaffected. Reported Vcmax25
  differs from the simple-Arrhenius convention of pyrealm and JULES by up to
  9 % (14 % for Jmax25), which matters only when comparing with trait
  observations.
* **Water viscosity.** Huber et al. (2009) in legoESM and pyrealm; an older
  empirical fit in JULES; ≤ 1.1 % in η*.

## 4. Evidence so far

### 4.1 The legoESM optimum reproduces pyrealm

On a grid of growth temperature (0–35 °C), VPD and pressure, with the same φ0
and light, legoESM and pyrealm agree to within 2 % at sea level in Γ*, K, ξ,
χ and Vcmax at the growth temperature. Fed legoESM's own 24-hour running
means, pyrealm reproduces legoESM's site trait series to 0.1–2.2 % (Vcmax25,
Jmax25, ξ). The larger deviations are at the two German sites, where the
pressure convention (DE-Obe at 734 m) and the 25 °C normalisation at cool
growth temperatures act as expected. The core optimum is therefore correctly
implemented, and the differences that remain are formulation choices, not
errors.

Relative to the current scheme at the same φ0, sub-daily acclimation raises
Vcmax25 by 1.76–2.04, Jmax25 by 1.56–1.85 and ξ by 1.13–1.33 (§3.1). The

drivers' prescribed values. Which φ0,ref is appropriate under sub-daily
acclimation is an empirical question for step B.

### 4.4 Step A implemented: the midday scheme reproduces pyrealm

legoESM's new `subdaily_noon` option, driven by the site forcing with the
same light and φ0,ref = 1/8, reproduces pyrealm's `SubdailyPModel` realised
traits at all four sites (summer medians, legoESM/pyrealm): Vcmax25
0.984–1.002, Jmax25 1.003–1.018, ξ 0.987–1.000. One window closed on every
one of the 1,461 days. The residuals have the structure expected from the
documented departures, not from implementation error. ξ and Vcmax25 fall
below pyrealm in order of site elevation (DE-Obe 734 m, 1.3 %; DE-Hai, 0.7 %;
the two US sites below 300 m, ≤ 0.2 %), which is the signature of the CLM
pressure convention for Kc and Ko. Jmax25 is 0.3–1.8 % higher, largest at the
cool German sites, which is the signature of normalising with Kattge & Knorr
(2007) rather than a simple Arrhenius function. With the default setting the
model is bit-identical to the original code.

| ------ | -------- | ---------------------- | ------------------ | --------------- |
| DE-Hai | 5.33     | 5.24 / 0.75            | 7.53 / 0.53        | 9.02 / 0.12     |
| DE-Obe | 4.93     | 4.13 / 0.64            | 6.25 / 0.49        | 7.71 / −0.15   |

| US-Ton | 2.76     | 2.45 / 0.78            | 3.93 / 0.45        | 5.05 / −0.67   |

Midday acclimation raises GPP by 40–60 % while Vcmax25 roughly doubles. As
expected, the canopy-scale response is smaller than the capacity change,
because shaded leaves and the low-light hours stay light-limited. LE rises by
7–9 W m⁻² and H falls by 4–7 W m⁻²; H improves at the three sites where it
was over-estimated. GPP, however, now exceeds observations at every site,
and the theoretical φ0,ref = 1/8 makes this worse.

**Interpretation.** Taken at face value, this would argue for keeping the
24-hour scheme. The more likely explanation is the compensation anticipated
in §3.2. The canopy kernel's light response has an initial quantum
efficiency of 0.5 × 0.85 / 4 ≈ 0.106 mol CO2 per mol absorbed photons,
independent of temperature. The P-model's φ0 at 25 °C is 0.0818 × 0.69 ≈
0.056 (or 0.086 with φ0,ref = 1/8). The kernel therefore turns light into
carbon about 1.9 times (1.2 times) more efficiently than the theory that
sizes the capacities assumes. With 24-hour acclimation, an under-sized
Vcmax offset this excess efficiency. With capacities sized as the theory
intends, the excess shows up as GPP. This is a prediction, not yet a
finding. Step D tests it directly: if the explanation is right, using φ0(T)
in the canopy's electron transport should remove most of the positive GPP
bias under midday acclimation, and do so mainly in the light-limited hours.

**Computational cost.** DE-Hai, prognostic, four-year record, one CPU (AMD
EPYC 9654), three interleaved repeats on the same node. Per-step cost is
taken as the difference between the full run and a one-day run, divided by
the step difference, which removes start-up and compilation.

| Setting                     | start-up + compilation | cost per half-hourly step |
| --------------------------- | ---------------------- | ------------------------- |
| prescribed traits           | 61.6 s                 | 2.278 ms                  |
| P-model, 24 h acclimation   | 63.8 s                 | 2.311 ms                  |
| P-model, midday acclimation | 65.6 s                 | 2.380 ms                  |

The midday scheme costs about 3 % more per step than the current P-model
(spread between repeats ±2 %) and about 2 s more to compile. The extra work
is the daily optimum, which is evaluated at every step and kept only when the
window closes; a branch that skipped it would not survive vectorisation over
columns, so this is close to the minimum for a differentiable implementation.
In a global run the column-independent costs (soil, energy balance) are the
same, so the relative overhead should be similar; this remains to be measured.

### 4.6 Step D: expectations written before the runs (2026-11-07)

Step D replaces the canopy's CLM light response by the P-model form,
J = 4φ0(T_leaf) I / √[1 + (4φ0 I / Jmax)²], with the same φ0(T) that sizes
Vcmax and Jmax. It is run on top of the midday acclimation (step A), so the
comparison is step A vs step A + D, everything else fixed. The kernel tests
confirm the mechanism in isolation: the ratio of initial light-use slopes
equals φ0(T)/0.10625 at 10 °C and 28 °C.

If the positive GPP bias of step A comes from the kernel's higher quantum
efficiency (§4.5), then:

1. GPP should fall relative to step A by roughly the ratio of initial
   yields in light-limited conditions: φ0/0.106 ≈ 0.53 at 25 °C for
   φ0,ref = 0.0818 and ≈ 0.81 for 1/8, less where leaves are light-saturated.
   Expected canopy-scale reduction: 20–40 % for 0.0818, 5–20 % for 1/8.
2. The reduction should be concentrated in low light (shaded leaves,
   mornings and evenings, overcast days), not at clear-sky midday.
3. The mean GPP bias should move from +1.2 to +2.2 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ (step A,
   0.0818) to within about ±0.5, and daily NSE should recover towards the
   current model's 0.64–0.79.

The explanation is refuted if GPP falls by less than 10 % for 0.0818, or if
the reduction is concentrated at high light. It is incomplete if the bias
stays above +0.5 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ while 1 and 2 hold. A control run of the
current model with every new switch at its default must reproduce the
baseline outputs bit for bit.

agreement rested on an under-sized Vcmax compensating a canopy light response
about twice as efficient as the P-model assumes. With midday acclimation and
the P-model light response together, the model reaches the same GPP skill,
now with capacities consistent with the theory and in the range of the site
trait estimates (§4.2). The theoretical φ0,ref = 1/8 still overestimates GPP
by 1.7–2.8 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, which leaves the level of φ0 as the open question for

the current model at the two German sites but remains 7–9 W m⁻² higher at
US-MMS and US-Ton, and H correspondingly lower at US-Ton. The likely reason is
stomatal: midday acclimation raises ξ, and hence the Medlyn slope, most at
hot sites (×1.33 at US-Ton, §4.2), increasing transpiration per unit carbon.
Step E (χ from the lagged ξ, without the canopy's minimum conductance
floor) bears directly on this.

### 4.8 Step C: implementation, site inputs and expectations

**Formulation.** φ0 = φ_peak(AI) · f(T; mGDD0) with the published constants,
in the JULES form including the factor T/Topt (user decision; pyrealm rc5
omits it in code, so legoESM/pyrealm-rc5 = T/Topt). Topt is the optimum of
the plain peaked Arrhenius function; with the T/Topt factor the maximum of φ0
lies 0.3–0.5 K above Topt and exceeds φ_peak by < 0.1 %. In legoESM,
f(Topt) = 1 holds exactly and the implementation reproduces the user's JULES
reference values at four sites to 10⁻⁴ (only the gas constant differs:
8.314462618 vs 8.3145). AI and mGDD0 are climatological constants per
column, set once at initialisation and carried in the acclimation state (and
restarts). φ0 is used in the optimum (at the acclimation temperature) and,
with step D, in the instantaneous electron transport at leaf temperature. C4
is unchanged, as in JULES. The global driver cannot yet select the method: a
global AI/mGDD0 map is needed (from CRU-JRA, or the WFDEI fields of the JULES
runs regridded), which is a separate decision.

**Site inputs.** Computed from each site's full record (user decision):
mGDD0 as the mean of daily-mean temperatures above 0 °C; AI as ΣPET/ΣP with
daily Priestley–Taylor PET (α = 1.26, s/(s+γ) from the canopy's own
saturation curve) on net radiation estimated from the forcing. Cross-check
against the JULES site ancillaries:

| ------ | ------------------ | --------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |

| US-MMS | 0.892 / 0.929      | 15.38 / 14.90               | 1.01                               |

mGDD0 agrees to within 1.4 °C. AI from four years of site forcing is lower

**Expectations, written before the runs.** At 20 °C the Sandoval φ0 with
these inputs is 0.084–0.107, i.e. 1.6–2.0 times the current form
(0.0818 × Bernacchi = 0.054) and 1.0–1.3 times the 1/8 form (0.082).

same factor relative to A and so raises GPP further above observations;
(2) A + C + D should give GPP close to, or slightly above, A + D with

smallest change at US-Ton, where aridity lowers the peak. If (2) holds, the
Sandoval quantum yield, like the theoretical 1/8, is too high for legoESM's
canopy as currently formulated, and the remaining excess must lie elsewhere
in the canopy (co-limitation smoothing, the sunlit/shaded capacity
scaling, stomatal behaviour; step E).

### 4.11 Step E: implementation and expectations

**Formulation.** A new stomatal model, `pmodel_chi`, follows JULES
`stomata_pmodel` and pyrealm's sub-daily step 8: at every step
ci = χ·Ca with χ = Γ*/Ca + (1 − Γ*/Ca)·ξ/(ξ + √D), from the acclimated
(or, under step A, realised) ξ, the current canopy-air VPD and Γ* at leaf
temperature; gs is diagnosed as 1.6·An/(Ca − ci), and closed stomata
(An ≤ 0, or gs below it) take the residual conductance b0, as JULES does
with glmin. As in JULES, the soil-moisture factor does not act on χ; in
legoESM it acts on Vcmax and therefore on An and gs. The χ formula is a
single function shared with the acclimated optimum. Tests confirm that an
open leaf's ci equals χ·Ca exactly and that sunlit and shaded leaves then
share ci/ca to within 0.02, unlike the Medlyn arm.

Departures from JULES: canopy-air VPD rather than reference-height VPD;
Γ* at leaf rather than air temperature (both the host kernel's own); the
co-limited FvCB kernel rather than min(Ac, Aj).

**Expectations, written before the runs.** Relative to A + D with Medlyn:
(1) where the canopy's ci/ca exceeded χ under Medlyn (US-Ton, §3.3) gs and
LE should fall, because b0 is no longer added on top of the optimal
conductance; (2) at mesic sites without strong water stress (US-MMS,
DE-Hai) LE should fall modestly where LE was over-estimated; (3) GPP should
change little (≤ 10 %), falling slightly where ci falls. Under drought two
effects oppose each other — Medlyn's slope is stressed whereas χ is not, but
b0 is no longer additive — so the sign of the LE change at US-Ton in the dry
season is not predicted.

### 4.14 Step F: canopy scaling consistent with the P-model (rationale and expectations)

**Why.** The coordination hypothesis that sizes Vcmax (§2.2) holds at every
depth in the canopy, not only at the top: capacity is expected to follow
the light each layer absorbs. The P-model's canopy form (Vcmax per ground
area from fAPAR·PPFD) and the JULES big-leaf scaling, (1 − e^(−kL))/k with
k = 0.5, both rest on that assumption. legoESM instead distributes the
P-model's top-leaf Vcmax25 with an empirical nitrogen profile far shallower
than the light profile, giving 2.1–2.7 times the JULES canopy capacity in the
closed canopies (§3.3b). Steps A–E made the leaf-level P-model consistent with
the theory and with JULES; this step makes the canopy integration consistent
too, using legoESM's existing light-following profile
(`canopy_vcmax_profile = 'coordination'`, `vcmax_light_frac = 1`, within
0–15 % of the JULES scaling). It is a test of the theory's canopy
assumption, not a claim that it is the better canopy model: observed
nitrogen gradients are shallower than light (de Pury & Farquhar 1997;
Niinemets), which is why legoESM's default fraction is 0.35.

**Design.** On top of A + D + E (prognostic soil, four sites), one variable
at a time: the profile (kn → coordination, f = 1) for φ0,ref = 0.0818, 1/8
and Sandoval; plus A + D + E with kn and 1/8, to complete the comparison.

**Expectations, written before the runs.** (1) In the closed canopies
(DE-Hai, DE-Obe, US-MMS) GPP falls, by less than the halving of canopy
capacity because most of the removed capacity is in shaded, light-limited
leaves: a 10–30 % reduction. (2) At US-Ton (LAI 1.1) the two profiles give
nearly the same capacity, so GPP changes by < 5 %. (3) If the overshoot of
1/8 and Sandoval (§4.9–4.10) comes from legoESM's extra canopy capacity,
then with the coordination profile those quantum yields move to within
about ±0.5 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ of the observed GPP at the closed sites, and
0.0818 becomes negatively biased. The explanation is refuted if the closed
canopies' GPP falls by less than 5 %.

   profiles carry the same capacity (LAI 1.1).
3. *The φ0 overshoot.* With the coordination profile the bias of 1/8 and
   Sandoval falls from +1.5 to +2.8 to +0.4 to +1.4 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ in the
   closed canopies, and 0.0818 becomes negatively biased (−0.3 to −1.3), as
   expected. The canopy over-capacity therefore accounts for one half to
   two thirds of the overshoot (DE-Hai 1.7 of 2.6, DE-Obe 1.0–1.1 of 1.5–1.6,
   US-MMS 1.3 of 2.7). The ±0.5 target is met only at DE-Obe, so the
   explanation is supported but incomplete: about +0.9 remains at DE-Hai and
   +1.4 at US-MMS, and at the open US-Ton site, where canopy scaling cannot
   matter, the overshoot (+1.2 to +1.8) is untouched.

**What this means.** Two configurations now reproduce the observed GPP at
the closed canopies to within about 1 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, for different reasons.
(a) Empirical nitrogen profile with φ0,ref = 0.0818 (bias −0.6 to +0.6,
daily NSE 0.66–0.78): the calibrated quantum yield compensates the extra
canopy capacity. (b) Fully theory-consistent — midday acclimation, Sandoval
φ0, P-model light response, χ from ξ and capacity following light (bias
+0.4 to +1.4; daily NSE 0.51–0.74, the best of the theory-based settings and
equal to the current model at DE-Hai). In (b) the remaining excess is largest
at the two sites with the highest observed water stress (US-MMS, US-Ton),
which points to the soil-moisture treatment (§5, later steps) rather than to
canopy integration. Under (b), LE rises with GPP (DE-Hai +8.9, US-MMS
+15.0 W m⁻²) and H falls correspondingly, improving H at the three sites
where it was over-estimated (daily H NSE 0.48, 0.13, 0.09 vs 0.43, −0.04,
−0.09 under (a)) at the cost of LE.

### 4.16 The cost of a global run, and where it goes

The global comparison (2° CRU-JRA, 16,200 columns of which 7,955 carry land,
hourly steps, 2000–2005) turned out to be limited by computing cost, not by
the P-model. On one LOTUS core a step costs about 80 s, i.e. 3.5–5 ms per
column-step, the same as a single flux-site column: running many columns
together buys no saving per column on CPU. Six years would take ~1,200 h. For
comparison, the JULES runs cost ~21 µs per land point and half-hourly step per
core, about 200 times less.

The time is in the canopy closure. Per step the two-leaf canopy solves six
coupled unknowns (sunlit and shaded leaf temperature and Ci, canopy-air
temperature and humidity) by damped Newton iteration, inside six outer passes
that couple it to the soil temperature. An iteration profile of every column,
every pass (24 steps from 1 January and from 30 June 2000) shows:

* the median land column converges in 5 iterations, but 10–14 % of land
  solves reach the 50-iteration cap and 18–21 % end unconverged; under one
  vectorised batch the whole batch then runs to the cap;
* ocean columns (no land, stepped all the same) cost ~29 iterations each;
* the six outer passes each restart cold, so passes 2–6 cost as much as pass 1;
* the unconverged solves are where there are no leaves: 49–55 % of
  ice-sheet and 17–36 % of barren solves, against 3–4 % of vegetated ones
  (§4.18); Antarctica alone was 55–73 % of all unconverged solves and 42–50 %
  of all iterations.

Two layout options were measured. More XLA threads in one process help
little (about 2.1× at 8 cores, 2.4× at 32). Splitting the independent columns
across CPU devices (`--column-devices N`, columns sharded by
`legoesm.parallel.column_shard`; results identical to round-off, tested) gives
6.4 s per step on 90 cores, about 12×. On one A100 a step costs ~5.9 s, and
four A100s give no further gain.

### 4.17 Porting the upstream canopy-solver speed-ups

legoESM main has three canopy-solver improvements that this branch predates
(it forked on 31 August): applying the QR factor from packed reflectors in
the Newton step (#2398), warm-starting the closure from the previous step's
solution together with a root certificate that rejects non-physical roots
(57e4c45b6a, 46bab7a21c), and solving columns in groups of 64 sorted by their
previous iteration count (#2330). They were ported as separate changes, with
the restart files carrying the warm-start cache and the drivers' NaN guards
taught that the cache is NaN where a column has never converged.

One finding matters for anyone running on CPU: the grouped, sorted solve was
designed for a single GPU, and with columns sharded across CPU devices it made
the run ~40 times slower (24 steps: 208 s → 2 h 27 min on 90 cores). Sorting
by iteration count gathers every column into one place, undoing the
sharding, and the groups then run in sequence.

With the leafless-column change (§4.18) the balance shifts on GPU too: once
ice-sheet and barren columns stop running to the iteration cap, one wide batch
is fastest. Per hourly step on one A100: one batch 0.73 s, groups of 1,024
1.0 s, groups of 64 5.7 s; the GPU is latency-bound (mean utilisation ~4 %,
2.7 of 40 GB), so sequential groups cost more than idle lanes, and splitting
the 2° columns over four GPUs did not help. The batching is therefore chosen
by platform (`canopy_solve_batching`, user decision 2026-11-10): one batch per
device on GPU and on column-sharded CPU; sorted groups of 64 only on a single
CPU device. Splitting over several GPUs is left for higher resolutions, where
each device carries enough work. GPU and CPU results agree to round-off
(daily GPP and latent heat, 99th percentile ~10⁻¹⁰).

### 4.18 No leaf physics where there are no leaves

**Why.** The two-leaf canopy solved leaf equations on every land column,
including those with no leaves at all: ice sheets (glacier-dominated cells,
LAI = 0 by construction) and barren ground. With no leaf area every leaf flux
vanishes (absorbed radiation and conductance scale with leaf area), so the two
leaf-temperature and two Ci equations carry no information. The Jacobian is
singular there (smallest singular value 10⁻²–10⁻¹), the leaf temperatures
drift without bound (to ~370 K in a probe of an ice column), and the column
never certifies a root. This, not cold or snow as such, is why the ice sheets
dominated the non-convergence and the cost (table below; iterations and
unconverged share per column-pass, 2° grid, January / July).

| Surface | Columns | Unconverged | Mean iterations |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ice sheet (glacier-dominated) | 2,059 | 49–55 % | 30–35 |
| No vegetation, not ice (LAI 0 all year) | 95 | 17–36 % | 27–32 |
| Sparse (LAI < 0.1 that month) | 990–2,140 | 18–24 % | 20–23 |
| Vegetated (LAI ≥ 0.1) | 3,660–4,810 | 3–4 % | 6–7 |

The same probe exposed a small physical artefact: on a leafless column each
leaf class carried an equal and opposite sensible and latent flux of
0.17 W m⁻² (dew forming on leaves that do not exist). It came from the
implied temperature of the leaf energy balance which, multiplied by the
vanishing leaf conductance, still exchanged heat and vapour with the canopy
air; the column's sensible heat to the atmosphere exceeded the ground's by
exactly that amount.

**What changed.** Where LAI ≤ 10⁻⁶ (a numerical zero, `leafless_mask`):

* the leaf unknowns lose their equations: leaf temperature = canopy-air
  temperature and Ci = Ca (the state of a leaf that takes up no CO2; any
  fixed value gives the same fluxes); under P-hydro the leaf water-potential
  draw-downs are zero;
* leaves exchange no heat or vapour with the canopy air, including the
  wet-leaf (intercepted-water) pathway;
* the reported leaf sensible, latent and wet-leaf fluxes, conductance, net
  radiation, assimilation and the light passed to SIF are zero.

The ground, canopy-air and atmosphere balance is solved exactly as before, so
bare soil and ice keep their full surface energy balance: soil temperature,
soil evaporation with its surface resistance and moisture limit, sensible and
ground heat, and radiation. The canopy-air node remains, as the near-surface
air through which the ground exchanges with the forcing height.

**Evidence.** Single-column probes (ice by night and day, hot and wet bare
soil) now certify in 2 iterations, where they ran to the 50–60-iteration cap.
Tests pin that the leaf state is fixed and the leaf fluxes are zero; that on
a leafless column all of the ground's sensible heat reaches the atmosphere
(ρc_p (T_c − T_a)/r_ah equals the ground's H to the solver tolerance; it was
off by 0.35 W m⁻² before); that the ground energy balance closes; that wet
leaves add no vapour; that the gradient of the ground's sensible heat stays
finite; and that vegetated (LAI 3) and sparse (LAI 10⁻³) columns are
bit-identical to the unchanged equations. Each of the five switches was
removed in turn and each removal fails at least one test. Codex reviewed the
change over four rounds (12–15): it found the wet-leaf pathway, the leaf net
radiation and carbon uptake just above zero leaf area, and the light passed to
SIF, all now covered; its concern that the 150–360 K admissibility box could
reject hot bare ground was withdrawn (the canopy air lies below the ground
skin, whose hottest retrieved value is ~344 K).

**Consequences and limits.** ci/ca reads 1 on leafless columns, and the
P-model traits are still advanced there (they feed nothing when LAI = 0): both
should be excluded from trait maps (LAI > 0). Sparse vegetation
(0 < LAI < 0.1) is unaffected by this change and still fails to converge in
about a fifth of its solves; that is a separate solver issue, recorded here
and not yet addressed.

## 5. Plan: one hypothesis per step

Each step changes one aspect of the formulation, is introduced as an option
with the current behaviour as default, and is evaluated against the previous
step with identical forcing, period and metrics.

**A. Sub-daily acclimation (Mengoli et al., 2022).** Optimum from each day's
noon-window means (11:00–13:00 local solar time: three hourly or five
half-hourly steps; only the noon step for coarser forcing), with a daily
memory α = 1/15 acting on ξ, Vcmax25 and Jmax25, in pyrealm's order.
*Expectation:* Vcmax25 roughly doubles (§4.2). GPP rises mainly at midday under
high light, by less than the change in capacity because shaded leaves and
low-light hours remain light-limited. LE rises through the coupling of gs to
assimilation. *Test:* trait parity with pyrealm on the site drivers (expected
within the departures of §3.4, ≈2.5 %), then site GPP, LE and H against the
baseline.

**B. φ0,ref under sub-daily acclimation.** Compare 0.0818 and 1/8.
*Expectation:* with sub-daily acclimation, 1/8 gives capacities well above the
site trait estimates (§4.2); 0.0818 brings them close. Whether the latter
reflects a better φ0 or a compensation is resolved in step D.

**C. Sandoval et al. (2026) quantum yield.** Requires AI and mGDD0 per site,
computed from the site forcing (Priestley–Taylor PET over precipitation;
daily mean temperature) and cross-checked against the WFDEI 1980–2010 fields
used in the JULES runs. *Expectation:* higher φ0 at warm, humid sites whose
growth temperature is close to the acclimated optimum; lower φ0 at arid sites
(US-Ton).

**D. φ0 in the instantaneous light response.** Under the P-model setting,
replace the canopy's fixed PSII-based light response with
J = 4φ0(T_leaf) I / √[1 + (4φ0 I/Jmax)²], as in JULES and pyrealm. This removes
the inconsistency of §3.2 between the light response that sizes the traits and
the one that uses them. *Expectation:* lower light-limited assimilation at
cool temperatures and in shade; the size of the change shows how much of the
current GPP agreement rests on the kernel's higher quantum efficiency.

**E. χ from the lagged ξ.** Option to set ci from ξ and the current VPD, as
JULES `stomata_pmodel` does, with gs diagnosed from A. *Expectation:* lower
ci/ca under drought where g0 currently dominates (§3.3), hence lower
transpiration at US-Ton.

Later, separately: soil-moisture formulations (Stocker et al., 2020;
Mengoli et al., 2023; Lavergne et al., 2020) and the Smith et al. (2019) Jmax
limitation.

## 6. Evaluation design

* **Sites.** DE-Hai, DE-Obe, US-MMS and US-Ton (example drivers from legoESM
  `main`), then the FLUXNET2015 sites used for the JULES evaluation. Primary
  mode: prognostic soil. Metrics: half-hourly bias, RMSE and r; daily NSE;
  mean diurnal cycle by season; summer trait means.
* **Global.** 2° LMIP with CRU-JRA forcing, scored with the ILAMB configuration
  used for the JULES P-model runs, with the JULES branch as a model reference.
* **Controls.** Before any comparison, the implementation must reproduce

  the change in traits that explains it.

  code too. One P-hydro test file aborts inside JAX after its ninth test on
  both the modified and the unmodified code (an out-of-memory condition on
  the shared login node); the full suite is run on a compute node instead.
